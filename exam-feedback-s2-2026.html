Part Four

What you did well

Describe and Construct are now genuine strengths. That is a reversal from Semester One, and it is worth naming before anything else – it is the evidence that the rest of this is fixable too.

Five things this paper got right

Overviews were tight. Most Construct responses named the work, director, year and the question’s concept in three to five sentences, without plot dumping.

Concepts were defined before being applied. Bias, representation and propaganda were mostly described in the abstract first, exactly as the study guide asks.

Terminology was secure. Section One terminology averaged 87%. Diegetic and non-diegetic sound, juxtaposition, mise-en-scène and framing were all used accurately.

The best evidence was construction-level. The strongest papers read details most viewers miss – the shop signs in the abandoned theme park, the CCTV montage’s running time, the architecture of the bathhouse.

Texts beyond the core two paid off. Responses on Samson and Delilah, a print advertising campaign, Whiplash and your own production were among the most original on the paper.

Part Six

What this paper showed

Four lessons are new to this exam. The first matters most – by some distance.

1
Highest valueEvidence

You have six texts – use them

You have studied four texts in depth – Samson and Delilah, Spirited Away, Bowling for Columbine and Waltz with Bashir – and each of you has two more from your investigation. On this paper, 33 of the 39 responses used just two of them: Spirited Away and Bowling for Columbine. Samson and Delilah appeared twice. Waltz with Bashir did not appear at all.

That cost marks in two ways. The same scenes – the Heston interview, the stink spirit, Yubaba’s penthouse – were bent to fit questions they did not suit. And four responses drawn from the same two films made papers read as repetitive, even when each answer was sound.

2
Reading time

Every question has its own object

The Evaluate bullets looked similar but asked different things. Question 7 asked about the intended audience. Question 8 asked about public understanding. Question 10 asked about audiences from different cultural contexts. A prepared Hall paragraph answers none of them exactly.

The fix: underline the object in reading time and write to it. The Semester Two Study Guide makes the same point in Part Five – different questions point Evaluate at values, at expectations, at understanding of an event, at public understanding of an issue. They are not interchangeable.

3
Content

A mood is not a theme

“Peace” or “tension” is an effect on the audience. A theme is an idea the work says something about – ambition and its cost, identity lost to consumerism.

The fix: if your “theme” is a feeling, a codes-construct-a-theme question caps your Content mark. This is the same note as the Semester One feedback, where the reflective stillness produced by ma was an effect and greed, consumerism and environmentalism were themes.

4
Structural

No question type is safe to skip

Nobody attempted Question 9 (technical and symbolic codes), and Question 3 (your own production) was barely touched. Both are standard WACE families.

This is now the third time this has come up. In the Semester One exam nobody attempted the values question. On Journal Entry 6 nobody took the Waltz with Bashir route. The pattern is not coincidence – it is the cost of revising only what you expect to be asked.

The fix: if either is set in November and your planned question is not, you need a route ready. Part Ten of this page tells you how to build one for Question 9 specifically.

Part Seven

Still open from earlier feedback

You have now seen these three in the Semester One feedback, in the Journal Entry 6 guide and in the study guide. If one of them is still you, it is your number one job – together they explain most of the gap between Section One and Section Two.

1 · Evaluate stops at analysis

Test each sentence: judgement or mechanism? Analyse and Evaluate averaged 62% and 61%, against 90% for Construct. If your last paragraph explains how a technique works, that is analysis – it belongs in the bullet before.

2 · Hall listed, not applied

Naming three reading positions earns little. Each needs an audience, their context, and the moment in the work that produces that reading. Example 1 below shows exactly what that looks like at 4/8 and at 7/8.

3 · Categories, not moments

“Selection and omission” or “archival footage” is a container. The marks are in the shot, the cut, and what sits next to what. This is the three-levels-of-evidence distinction from the Journal Entry 6 guide, and it is still the most common reason an Analyse bullet stalls at 4 or 5.

Part Eight

Examples from the paper

Both extracts come from this exam, anonymised and lightly corrected for spelling only. The first is the single most useful comparison on this page: the same film and the same bullet, written at 4/8 and at 7/8.

Example 1 – Evaluate at 4 and at 7

Question 10 · Evaluate the extent to which audiences from different cultural contexts would interpret the media work differently, using a media theory to support your reasoning. (8 marks)
Evaluate · 4/8Spirited Away
An audience that may see the preferred reading is an older Japanese audience, or one similar to Hayao Miyazaki himself. They see Spirited Away as a coming-of-age film that criticises consumerism, pollution and identity loss. This older Japanese audience will read it this way as they have lived through similar circumstances to Miyazaki. An audience with a negotiated reading could be someone who likes watching coming-of-age films. They read Spirited Away as a coming-of-age film as they recognise that there is clear character development in Chihiro…
What’s limiting it: the reading positions are named, but “someone who likes coming-of-age films” is a taste, not a cultural context. No moment in the film produces either reading, and there is no verdict on the extent.
Evaluate · 7/8Spirited Away
The dominant reading Miyazaki attempts to convey is that the class imbalance Western capitalist culture creates is not only negative, but also responsible for the oppression of traditional Japanese culture. However, some Western audiences may have a negotiated reading, missing the specifically Western difference of Yubaba, as to them it appears normal, if not a bit tacky. Their reading may still recognise the capitalist critique, but the critique of globalisation may be missed through a lack of cultural context. Some from Western cultures may even have an oppositional reading, as their cultural context aligns their values with capitalism, so they may see Yubaba as entrepreneurial and working the system to her advantage, entirely negating the critique.
What’s lifting it: each audience is defined by its cultural context, each reading is tied to a specific element (Yubaba’s Western dress and penthouse), and the oppositional reading is taken seriously rather than dismissed. It also separates two critiques – capitalism and globalisation – and shows that one travels across cultures and the other does not.
The last mark7/8 → 8/8
The critique of greed therefore survives translation; the critique of Westernisation largely does not, which limits how far the film’s meaning is shared.
Say it outright. That one sentence is the verdict and the limit – the two components Extract B is missing. Everything else in Extract B is already there. This is what the Evaluate clinic means by “an explicit verdict” and “a stated limit”.
Do this with your own paper

Take the Evaluate paragraph you actually wrote. Keep the analysis exactly as it is. Add a second audience with its own evidence, one verdict sentence, and one limit. That is the whole distance between Extract A and Extract B, and then between B and full marks.

Example 2 – Unpacking one scene

Question 4 · Discuss how a media producer has presented an issue in a media work you have studied. (12 marks – this response scored 12/12)
Section One · 12/12Bowling for Columbine
In the scene “North Country Bank, Michigan – free gun with your account”, Moore presents himself through the plain folks technique to establish trust and affinity with his audience, characterised by his casual dress, awkward countenance and “just a normal guy” presentation. Editorial control, selection and omission allow a neat, overly convenient narrative in the bank, omitting the actual complexity of acquiring a gun in the US. Instead, the process is shown through editing as: walk in → open account → request gun → background check → receive firearm → walk out. What would be a multiple-week process is cut into what appears to be a single day, with Moore wearing the same outfit to display continuity.
The move: one named scene, read at the level of construction – persona, then the edit itself, then continuity in costume. The student does not say Moore “uses editing”; they show the six-step cut and what it leaves out. That is the difference between Level 2 and Level 3 evidence.

Final thoughts

You already know these films well.
