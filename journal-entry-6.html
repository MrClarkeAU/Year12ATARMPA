Naming a topic where the question asks for a concept

The single most expensive habit across the cohort, and it turned up in every bullet. Gun violence is an issue, not an ideology. Gun ownership is a practice, not a value. “Equality”, “empathy” and “truth” are virtues, not the values this documentary contests.

An ideology is a system of belief. A value is something a person holds to be worth protecting. When a question asks for one of those, the mark is for naming the concept – not the subject matter it attaches to. Use the strike test below, and drill the sorter underneath it until the distinction is automatic.

2
StructuralCaps the top bands

Answering the four bullets as four separate questions

The four bullets are one argument that builds. The point of view you analyse in bullet 3 is the ideological position you established in bullet 1. The values you evaluate in bullet 4 are the ones you defined in bullet 2.

Where a response treated each bullet as a standalone question, the later criteria had nothing to attach to and could not reach the upper bands however well written they were. Plan the argument once, then let each bullet do its share of it.

3
EvidenceFive vs seven

Pointing at evidence instead of producing it

This was everywhere, and it is the difference between a five and a seven. “The disturbing imagery of the Columbine High School massacre…” – which imagery? The cafeteria security camera footage? The 911 audio? The photographs of the victims?

Naming the category of evidence is not evidence. Naming the instance is. The same applies to “archival footage”, “the animation”, “interviews” and “satire”. Each of those is a container. The marks are for what is inside it.

4
Analytical stanceTop band only

Being persuaded by the media work instead of analysing it

Several responses reported the film’s own rhetorical self-presentation as fact: that Moore is “evaluating from both sides”, or presenting “only facts instead of personal opinions”. He is doing neither.

The film is built to produce that impression, and noticing how it is built is the analysis. Repeating it is evidence that the technique worked on you. The sharpest version of this in the whole cohort: one response described a cut as “confirming” an encounter to be legitimate. The cut is not confirming anything – it is manufacturing the appearance of confirmation. That is the observation the criterion rewards.

The strike test

Delete the word “ideology” or “values” from your sentence and read what is left. If the sentence collapses, you named a topic. Press the button and watch it happen.

“Moore challenges audience values on gun ownership.”
→ “challenges audience gun ownership” Gun ownership is a practice, not a value. The sentence collapses, so it was never naming one. Fix: “challenges the audience value of security through firearm ownership”.
“Moore challenges the ideology of American gun violence.”
→ “challenges American gun violence” Gun violence is a phenomenon, an issue. The paragraph only looked like it had done the job because an ideology-shaped noun was sitting in the ideology-shaped slot. Fix: “the ideology that an armed citizen is a free citizen”.
“The media work challenges the dominant American ideology that an armed citizen is a free and safe citizen.”
→ “challenges that an armed citizen is a free and safe citizen” Still stands up. A belief is being named, not a subject. This one passes.

Part 3

The three levels of evidence

This is the most useful distinction in the whole guide. Most responses sat at level two and thought they were at level three. Here is the same example – the James Nichols interview – written all three ways.

Level 1 · Plot recall
“Moore interviews James Nichols, a pro-gun ideologist and the brother of a convicted terrorist.”

This is what happens in the media work. It is not evidence for a construction criterion at all.

Level 2 · Content recall
“Nichols speaks rapidly and seems agitated, which makes him look unstable.”

Better, because there is an effect attached. But you are describing a person, not a producer’s decisions. This is where most of the cohort sat.

Level 3 · Construction — this is where the marks are
“The interview is staged in Nichols’ own kitchen, and the mise-en-scène does the work before he has spoken: the room is a mess, old newspapers and plates of food strewn through the frame. Moore then cuts repeatedly to close-ups that isolate Nichols’ darting eyes and constant rocking, and the frenetic pace of his speech is left unsmoothed by the edit. Where to film, what to keep in frame, when to cut in, what to leave in the audio – every one of these is a producer’s choice, and together they construct a representation of mental instability that invites the audience to discount whatever he says.”

That third version is the standard. It is adapted from a 2025 WACE script that scored very highly, and it is worth noticing that it contains not a single word about what Nichols actually says.

The four questions that get you to level 3

Ask these of every example you use: What did the producer choose? What did they leave out? What did they put next to what? How is it framed and cut?

If your sentence answers none of those, you are at level 1 or 2 – however confident it sounds.

Question 1 – bullet by bullet Bowling for Columbine

Bullet 1 · the cohort’s most consistent structural failure
Construct an overview of a media work that challenges dominant ideologies
3 marks

The two-part rule

The cause of failure here was identical nearly every time: responses did one half of the job and not the other. Some established the media work beautifully and never named an ideology. Others named the ideology precisely and never told the marker what the documentary actually is or covers. A 3 needs both, and the ones who scored 3 did both.

MarkWhat it takes
3Constructs an overview. The media work is established – what it is, what it covers, how it is made – and the specific dominant ideology it challenges is named, with the challenge as the organising idea.
2Provides relevant comments. Accurate and relevant, but one half is missing, implied, or generalised.
1Limited or superficial comment. Topic or plot summary; the question’s angle is not engaged.

Two ways to lose the mark

1 · Naming the subject matter instead of the ideology

“Moore challenges the ideology of American gun violence” – gun violence is a phenomenon, not an ideology. The paragraph looks like it has done the job because an ideology-shaped noun is sitting in the ideology-shaped slot. Run the strike test in Part 2 over your own opening sentence.

2 · Attributing the challenge to the director rather than the media work

“Michael Moore challenges the dominant ideology…” – the question asks about a media work that challenges dominant ideologies. A documentary is a constructed object: shot, cut, scored and sequenced. Writing only about what its director believes moves the frame off construction at exactly the point you should be establishing it – and it predicts the evidence problem that follows in bullets 3 and 4.

What a 3 looks like

“Bowling for Columbine (Moore, 2002) is a participatory documentary that investigates the causes of American gun violence through the 1999 Columbine High School shooting and the culture surrounding it. The media work challenges the dominant American ideology that an armed citizen is a free and safe citizen – a belief rooted in individualism, self-reliance and the constitutional right to bear arms, and one which presents itself as common sense rather than as belief. Through selection, omission, juxtaposition and satire, the documentary relocates the cause of gun violence away from individual pathology and towards a culture of fear manufactured and circulated by news media, government and commerce.”
What that paragraph does in three sentences: establishes the work and its form; names the ideology and what it rests on; states the nature of the challenge. It could not preface any other question – which is the test of whether an overview is really an overview.
Bullet 2 · the cohort’s strongest criterion
Describe the concept of ideology
4 marks
This went better than expected

Describe was the cohort’s strongest criterion on this task – a genuine reversal from Semester One, where it was the systematic weak spot. Whatever changed in how you are approaching definitional questions, it worked.

The four features

A full-mark description of ideology contains four things. Most responses had two.

  • Shared and collective – held by a group or a society, not an individual opinion.
  • Naturalised – it appears as common sense, as “just the way things are”, rather than as a belief that could be otherwise. This is the feature that distinguishes ideology from opinion, and it is the one most often missing.
  • Circulated through institutions – media, government, education, commerce. Ideologies are not simply held; they are reproduced.
  • Serving particular interests – ideologies are not neutral. They benefit somebody.
MarkWhat it takes
4Describes the concept. A shared system of beliefs and values, naturalised as the norm, circulated through institutions including the media, serving particular interests.
3Outlines the concept. The group dimension and one or two further features, with naturalisation or interests missing.
2Limited or superficial comment. “Ideology is what people believe”, plus an example.
1Identifies elements. Names a belief or an -ism without defining the concept.

The trap

Defining by applying

The bullet asks for the concept. Describing American gun ideology is applying the concept, not defining it. Several responses gave one thin clause of definition and then spent the rest of the paragraph on the film. Define first, in its own right, then illustrate.

Also worth getting straight

Ideology and dominant ideology are not the same thing. An ideology does not have to be widely accepted. A dominant one is. Say both.

What a 4 looks like

“An ideology is a shared system of beliefs, values and assumptions held by a group or society, which organises how its members understand the world and what they take to be normal. Its defining characteristic is that it does not present itself as a belief at all: a dominant ideology is naturalised, appearing as common sense rather than as one position among several. Ideologies are circulated and reproduced through institutions – news media, government, education and commerce – and they are never neutral, because the assumptions they naturalise serve some interests more than others. In the United States the belief that firearm ownership guarantees personal security functions ideologically in exactly this way: it is held across the culture, treated as self-evident rather than arguable, reinforced through news coverage, advertising and children’s play, and profitable to the industries that depend on it.”
The move: all four features are defined in the abstract across three sentences, and only then does the film’s context arrive – as illustration, not as substitute. Notice the last sentence walks back through all four features in order.

Breadth of instance beats breadth of technique

Choose depth

A response that names six techniques with no located example scores badly. A response that pursues one technique family across six located examples, and then says what the pattern adds up to, can score at the very top.

The technique bank

Selection and omission – the strongest single lens on this film, and the one that rewards depth most. The Charlton Heston sequence is the showcase: the “from my cold dead hands” clip is intercut with the Denver rally speech as though they are one address. It is not. That footage was recorded a year later at an unrelated event, and the clips Moore uses are assembled from seven separate sentences. Watch Heston’s suit change between shots. Moore also omits the passages where Heston expresses regret and confirms the rally was scaled back.

Juxtaposition and the smash cut – the Lockheed Martin sequence. A spokesperson denies any connection between the intercontinental ballistic missiles built on the edge of Littleton and the violence at Columbine; Moore cuts straight from that denial into the “What a Wonderful World” montage of American military interventions abroad, each labelled on screen with location, date and death toll. The argument is made entirely through arrangement. No narration is required.

Animation and visual rhetoric – “A Brief History of the United States of America” traces American gun culture to white fear of Black Americans and draws a direct line from the Ku Klux Klan to the NRA. The comedic register is doing the work: it simplifies a contested historical claim into something an audience will accept in ninety seconds.

Diegetic against non-diegetic sound – the 911 calls, the surveillance footage and the interview responses are diegetic and authenticate the material as real; the music and narration are non-diegetic and direct how you are meant to feel about it. The credibility of the first is used to carry the argument of the second. This is one of the most sophisticated observations available on this film and almost nobody reached it.

Persona and the “plain folks” appeal – the baseball cap, the loose shirt, the hands in pockets, the deliberate air of being intimidated. Moore constructs himself as an ordinary American so that his conclusions arrive as common sense rather than as argument. This is a named propaganda technique, and using its name earns terminology credit.

Pinpointing the enemy – a diffuse social problem is reduced to identifiable antagonists: the NRA, Heston, K-Mart. Giving an audience something to be against unites them, and a united audience questions less.

Testimonial, transfer, card stacking, bandwagon, fear appeal, exaggeration, satire and irony – all present, all nameable, all worth knowing by name.

The pattern is the argument

This is the move that separates a very good response from an outstanding one, and nobody in the cohort made it. Analysing three or four instances of selection is good. Noticing what the pattern of selection amounts to is better, because it turns a list of examples into a thesis. Look at who Moore chooses to film and how each is framed:

  • James Nichols – filmed in a squalid kitchen, cut in close-up on darting eyes, speech left frantic.
  • The police officer who strapped a gun to a beagle – included as an anecdote for no reason other than to make law enforcement look ridiculous.
  • Charlton Heston – the only articulate pro-gun figure, and even he is cut into aggression and single-mindedness.
  • Barry Glassner, the taxi drivers, ordinary family folk – anti-gun voices, all articulate, composed and entirely normal.
  • Marilyn Manson – the most divisive figure in the film, presented calm, thoughtful and educated.
The structural claim

Every pro-gun interviewee is constructed as unstable or foolish. Every anti-gun interviewee is constructed as reasonable. That is not six separate decisions – it is a single sustained casting strategy, and stating it as such is a structural claim about the media work rather than a list of observations about it.

The Manson inclusion is the keystone: by making the culture’s designated lunatic the most reasonable person on screen, Moore implies that the people blaming him are the mad ones.

What separates a 6 from a 7

A 6 discusses two or three techniques with detailed evidence. A 7 does that and shows the mechanism – not merely that the technique produces an effect, but how the construction produces it. The strongest response in the cohort reached 7 by noticing Heston’s suit changes between shots and using that to expose the edit. That is not recall about the film; it is reading the frame for the evidence of its own construction.

What stopped it going higher was scope: it pursued one technique across one subject. The pattern claim above is what turns the same technique into a top-band answer.

What a 7 looks like

“Moore’s most sustained persuasive strategy is selection, and it operates less through any single sequence than through a casting pattern maintained across the whole media work. Every pro-gun interviewee is constructed as unstable or foolish: James Nichols is filmed in a squalid kitchen and cut in close-up on his darting eyes and constant rocking; a police officer is included solely to recount strapping a gun to a beagle. Every anti-gun voice – Barry Glassner, taxi drivers, ordinary family folk – is composed, articulate and entirely unremarkable. The keystone is Marilyn Manson, the figure the culture had already designated a lunatic, whom Moore presents as the calmest and most thoughtful person on screen, so that the audience concludes the people blaming him are the irrational ones.”
“The Heston material shows the same strategy operating at the level of the cut. Moore presents Heston raising a rifle and declaring his guns will be taken ‘from my cold dead hands’, positioning this as the NRA’s response to Columbine, and the edit invites the viewer to read it as continuous with the Denver rally address. It is not: that footage was recorded a year later at an unrelated event, Heston is wearing a different suit between shots, and the clips are assembled from seven separate sentences. Moore compounds this by omitting the passages in which Heston expresses regret and confirms the rally was scaled back. By reducing a diffuse ideology to identifiable antagonists and then constructing each of them as irrational, Moore gives his audience someone to be against – and an audience that has been handed a target is far less likely to interrogate the position it has been handed with it.”
The move: paragraph one makes the structural claim and evidences it across five located instances. Paragraph two drops to a single sequence and reads the frame itself for proof of the edit. Pattern, then mechanism – and both are about producer decisions, never about what anyone says.

Where the cohort lost most heavily

Evaluate accounted for roughly half of all marks lost on this task, and the reason is specific and fixable: most responses answered bullet 4 with a second bullet 3. They produced more analysis of construction where the criterion asked for a judgement about effect.

The diagnostic question

Read your final paragraph and ask: does this contain a judgement, or only a mechanism? If it explains how a technique works, that is analysis, and it belongs in bullet 3. Bullet 4 asks whether it worked, on whom, and what that tells us.

“Describes” earns 4 on Analyse but only 3 on Evaluate. That is deliberate: evaluation is the higher-order skill. There is a side-by-side comparison of the two ladders directly below this accordion.

The structural gate – the upper bands need both of these

1. Theoretical scaffolding. Hall’s encoding and decoding is the natural fit, and the cohort used it far better than in Semester One. Two cautions. First, apply it rather than naming it – a sentence that says “according to Hall’s theory” and then stops has gestured at theory, not used it. Second, get the typology right:

  • Dominant (or preferred) – the audience accepts the meaning the producer encoded.
  • Negotiated – the audience broadly accepts it but modifies it against their own experience.
  • Oppositional – the audience understands the preferred meaning perfectly well and rejects it, decoding from a contrary frame.
The one term the cohort consistently mangled

Oppositional does not mean “reads it differently” or “gets something else out of it”. It means the viewer has decoded the preferred meaning accurately and refused it.

2. Differentiated reading positions. The strongest responses run all three, and give each one different material from the media work to stand on. Naming three positions and supporting them with the same example is not differentiation.

Where outside research belongs

The Analyse criterion asks for evidence from the media work. The resistant reading does not, and cannot, because it is by definition constructed from what the viewer knows that the film chose not to show.

This is the one place in the response where researched material outside the media work earns marks rather than wasting them. For the Kayla Rolland sequence, Moore omits that the six-year-old shooter’s father was in prison, that the boy was living in a crackhouse where his uncle traded guns for drugs, that he had a documented history of behavioural incidents including stabbing another student with a pencil, and that he had threatened Kayla the day before.

Two cautions on using material like this

Deploy it in the evaluation, not the analysis – it is evidence about the gap between the film and the record, not about construction.

Do not let it collapse into “Moore is a liar” – the strongest version holds both truths at once: that these details are themselves rooted in the societal injustices Moore is describing, and that withholding them removes layers of accountability the audience needed in order to understand the event.

Three judgements available to you

1 · On efficacy and audience knowledge

The film’s persuasive power is inversely proportional to what the viewer already knows. It is extremely effective on an audience that does not seek out corroborating information, and progressively less effective the more context the viewer holds. There is a real irony worth stating: the film depends for its effect on precisely the passive, under-informed audience condition that it criticises the mass media for producing.

2 · On arrangement versus fabrication – and the case that tests it

The usual judgement is that Moore’s manipulation lies in arrangement rather than invention: the interviews and archival footage are genuine, and the distortion occurs through selection, omission, ordering and juxtaposition. That holds across most of the media work, and it is a more sophisticated criticism than accusing him of making things up.

But do not stop there, because the Heston sequence is the case where the distinction breaks. Clips drawn from seven separate sentences, recorded a year later at a different event, are not authentic material arranged suggestively – they are an utterance assembled from parts, and Heston never delivered it. Identifying the point at which a general rule fails is a stronger evaluative move than applying the rule, and it is available to you here.

3 · On discourse versus attitude

The film has demonstrably contributed to a shared discourse about gun ownership – it changed how the issue is discussed, especially outside the United States – while having had comparatively little effect on the pre-established attitudes of Americans who already held a position. Shifting the conversation and shifting beliefs are different achievements, and distinguishing them is exactly the kind of judgement the top band is looking for.

The universal-decodability trap

These cap your mark where it costs most

“Moore convinces the audience that…”  ·  “this makes audiences want to change society”  ·  “a masterpiece”.

Every one of these claims that all viewers decode the media work identically, which is the opposite of what the criterion asks. If a sentence in your final paragraph could not survive the question “which audience?”, rewrite it.

What a 6–7 looks like

“The techniques Moore employs challenge the values of self-reliance and security-through-ownership that underpin the pro-gun position, but whether that challenge succeeds depends almost entirely on what the viewer brings to the film. An audience with limited prior knowledge of the NRA is positioned to decode the preferred reading: the Heston sequence constructs a callous antagonist, and there is nothing within the media work itself to disturb that impression. A viewer who knows the material well decodes it very differently. Recognising that the ‘cold dead hands’ footage has been lifted from a separate speech, this audience understands precisely what Moore intends and refuses it – an oppositional reading in Hall’s sense – and may leave the documentary with their pro-gun position hardened, because the manipulation appears to confirm that the case against them requires distortion to be made. This is the central limit of Moore’s method: the persuasive power of the film depends on a comparatively uninformed audience, which is precisely the audience condition the documentary itself attributes to the American mass media. The film has succeeded in shaping how gun ownership is discussed, particularly beyond the United States; it has been markedly less successful at changing the minds of the Americans whose values it set out to challenge.”
The move: every sentence after the first is a judgement, not a mechanism. Two reading positions each stand on different material. Hall is used, not cited. And the final sentence separates discourse from attitude – a genuine evaluative distinction, delivered in a single clause.

Part 5

Question 2 – the route nobody took Waltz with Bashir

No one attempted Question 2 with Waltz with Bashir in this task. Both students who chose Question 2 used Bowling for Columbine instead. That is worth knowing, because Moore’s film does not satisfy this question.

The qualifier that decides everything

Question 2 asks about an “alternative, experimental or non-commercial” media work. “Alternative” describes where a media work sits in relation to mainstream production and distribution. It does not mean “oppositional in argument” or “unconventional in style”.

Bowling for Columbine had major studio distribution, won an Academy Award and was, at the time, the highest-grossing documentary ever made. Participatory mode is one of the established documentary modes, not a departure from them. A media work can be radical in its politics and entirely mainstream in its form and distribution – and this one is.

Why it costs so much: defining “alternative” by stance and then using your own definition to license the choice is the trap, and it bleeds marks across all four bullets, because every band descriptor in Question 2 is qualified by that phrase.

Why Waltz with Bashir qualifies

Form An animated documentary. Not rotoscoped, as is often assumed – the animators used Flash combined with cut-out animation over four years, built from filmed interviews that were scripted, re-recorded and then drawn.
Mode Reflexive – the film is about the act of recovering memory and shows its own investigative process. Performative – Folman’s own unreliable memory is the vehicle for the truth claim rather than an obstacle to it.
Production position Israeli, Hebrew-language, roughly a two-million-dollar budget, produced by a small independent consortium.
Bullet 1
Construct an overview of an alternative media work
3 marks

Three jobs, not two this time. Establish the media work, and name what makes it alternative, and name the dominant perspective it challenges.

The move that lifts it

Connect the form to the challenge. Animating recovered memory is what allows the film to contest an account that the historical record had settled. The alternative form is not a stylistic footnote – it is the reason the challenge is possible at all. Say that in the overview and every later bullet has something to build on.

Bullet 2 · the baseline is compulsory
Describe dominant perceptions presented in mainstream media
4 marks
Read the bullet exactly

This bullet asks what mainstream media presented – not what the alternative work says. A response that describes only the alternative work’s perspective, leaving the mainstream account implied, cannot pass the middle of the ladder.

For Waltz with Bashir: the settled mainstream account of the 1982 Lebanon War and the Sabra and Shatila massacre, in which some 2,000 civilians were killed, positions the killings as a Phalangist atrocity and Israeli forces as bystanders rather than as participants in the conditions that enabled it.

The order to write it in: describe that account → attribute it to mainstream coverage → then let the film contest it.

Bullet 3 · the codes must be the alternative ones
Analyse the use of alternative codes and conventions
8 marks
The qualifier again

The codes you analyse must be the alternative ones. A competent reading of a conventional technique does not serve this criterion however good it is.

Colour as an epistemic marker

Colour tells you what kind of truth you are looking at: desaturated realism for the talking-head interviews, washed-out brown for the 1973 Yom Kippur War, blue for the Second World War, and an orange-grey scheme that signals the factical against a monochromatic grey that signals the factual. The motivator scene – soldiers rising naked from a flare-lit sea off Beirut – appears at the eighth minute and again in full at the sixtieth, and the colour shift is the only clue that resolves it.

Sound against image

Soldiers surf under fire and fire RPGs at civilians while “Yesterday I Bombed Beirut” plays. Frenkel dances his “insane waltz” with a machine gun through a Beirut street to Chopin’s Waltz in C-sharp minor. The disjunction between uplifting score and atrocity charges each sequence with irony and makes the argument about the absurdity of war without stating it.

Reflexivity

The trauma specialist describes a soldier who survived combat by watching it through an imaginary camera until, confronted with a field of slaughtered horses, the camera broke. The film is describing its own method – and inviting you to ask whether animation is doing for the viewer what the imaginary camera did for the soldier.

The rupture into live action

The single most analysable decision in the media work

The closing seconds hold on Folman’s face, then cut to real footage of the women whose voices have been superimposed on the animation. Sound bridges the transition – the film’s only use of location audio over animated images – and the cut is deliberately sharp.

Why it matters: it withdraws the animation’s protection at exactly the moment the viewer most needs it.

Bullet 4 · understanding of an event, not values
Evaluate the effect on audience understanding
8 marks
Keep the object straight

The object here is audience understanding of an event – what the audience comes to know or believe – not values. That is the difference between this bullet and Question 1’s bullet 4. Do not import the wrong one.

The strongest available line runs through representation

The Palestinians in this film have no names and no speech; they are anonymous, and the camera lingers on dead faces. The victims of Waltz with Bashir are the Israeli soldiers: it is their anguish, confusion and guilt that the film humanises across ninety minutes.

In the closing live-action footage a woman screams “my son, my son” and “take photos, take photos” in Arabic – and her words are not subtitled. That is a directorial choice.

The judgement to put to the marker

Does the film challenge audience understanding of Sabra and Shatila, or does it reinforce an account in which Israeli memory is the story and Palestinian suffering is its backdrop?

The film’s own psychologist supplies the line to read it against: “we don’t go to places we don’t want to; memory takes us where we want to go.” Read against the grain, the remembering this film undertakes may not take its audience anywhere they would truly rather not go.

That is a genuine evaluative judgement, it is supported by located evidence, and it is available to any student who has watched the last three minutes carefully.

Part 6

The moves bank

Every transferable move in this guide, pulled out of its paragraph and stated so you can use it on a different media work in November. Filter by the criterion you want to work on.

Construct Bullet 1
“Could this paragraph preface any other question?”
The move: an overview that would work as the opening of any essay on that film is not an overview – it is a synopsis. A real overview establishes the work and names the specific ideology, with the challenge as the organising idea, so that it could only introduce this question.
Construct · framing Bullet 1
“The media work challenges…” — not “Michael Moore challenges…”
The move: make the constructed object the subject of your sentence, not the director. A documentary is shot, cut, scored and sequenced. Putting the work in the subject position keeps you on construction from the first line – and the responses that started with the director kept drifting off construction in bullets 3 and 4.
Describe Bullet 2
“Define first, in its own right. Then illustrate.”
The move: describing American gun ideology is applying the concept, not defining it. Write the concept in the abstract – shared, naturalised, institutionally circulated, interest-serving – and only then reach for the film. The illustration should walk back through the features in the same order.
Analyse · evidence Bullet 3
“What did the producer choose, what did they leave out, what did they put next to what, and how is it framed and cut?”
The move: four questions that convert level-2 content recall into level-3 construction evidence. If your sentence answers none of them, you are describing the film rather than analysing it – however confident it sounds.
Analyse · the top-band move Bullet 3 · 8
“That is not six separate decisions – it is a single sustained casting strategy.”
The move: state what the pattern of your examples amounts to. Several instances plus a structural claim beats several instances alone, because it turns a list into a thesis. Nobody in the cohort made this move – it is the cleanest available route from a 7 to an 8.
Analyse · mechanism Bullet 3 · 6→7
“Heston is wearing a different suit between shots.”
The move: read the frame for the evidence of its own construction. This is not recall about the film – it is a detail visible on screen that exposes the edit. It is what took the strongest response in the cohort from a 6 to a 7.
Evaluate · the gate Bullet 4
“Does this contain a judgement, or only a mechanism?”
The move: the diagnostic to run over your final paragraph. If it explains how a technique works, that is analysis and it belongs in bullet 3. Bullet 4 asks whether it worked, on whom, and what that tells us. Answering bullet 4 with a second bullet 3 is a one-mark penalty on eight marks before anything else is assessed.
Evaluate · differentiation Bullet 4
“Three reading positions, and different material from the media work under each one.”
The move: naming dominant, negotiated and oppositional and supporting all three with the same example is not differentiation. Dominant stands on the casting pattern; negotiated on Kayla Rolland; oppositional on the omissions. Different evidence per position is what the upper bands are actually checking for.
Evaluate · sophistication Bullet 4 · 8
“The Heston sequence is the case where the distinction breaks.”
The move: establish a general rule – Moore manipulates through arrangement, not invention – then identify the point at which your own rule fails. Finding the exception to your own argument is a stronger evaluative move than applying the argument, and it reads as sophistication rather than inconsistency.
Evaluate · distinguishing achievements Bullet 4 · 8
“It changed the discourse. It did not change the attitudes.”
The move: split one vague success claim into two different achievements and rate them separately. Shifting how an issue is discussed and shifting what people believe are not the same thing, and saying so is exactly the kind of judgement the top band rewards.
Watch out · universal decodability Bullet 4
“Moore convinces the audience that…”  ·  “this makes audiences want to change society”  ·  “a masterpiece”
What is wrong: each one claims all viewers decode the media work identically – the opposite of what the criterion asks. Test every sentence in your final paragraph against the question “which audience?” If it cannot survive that, rewrite it.
Watch out · persuaded, not analysing Bullet 3
“The cut confirms the encounter to be legitimate.”
What is wrong: the cut is not confirming anything – it is manufacturing the appearance of confirmation. Reporting the film’s own rhetorical self-presentation as fact (“evaluating from both sides”, “only facts, not opinions”) is evidence that the technique worked on you. Noticing how the impression is built is the analysis.
Watch out · the container Every bullet
“The disturbing imagery of the Columbine High School massacre…”
What is wrong: which imagery? The cafeteria security camera footage, the 911 audio, the photographs of the victims? “Archival footage”, “the animation”, “interviews” and “satire” are all containers. The marks are for what is inside them.
Watch out · Hall’s typology Bullet 4
“An oppositional reading is when the audience gets something else out of it.”
What is wrong: the one term the cohort consistently mangled. Oppositional means the viewer understands the preferred meaning perfectly well and rejects it, decoding from a contrary frame. “Reads it differently” is closer to negotiated – and naming a theory you have misdefined costs more than not naming it.
