Not all marks are worth the same

This is worth thirty seconds of your attention because almost nobody works it out. Twenty-four raw marks become 30% of the written exam; forty-six become 70%. Do the division.

30%SECTION ONE · 30 MIN
70%SECTION TWO · 120 MIN

Section One buys you 1% of the written exam per minute. Section Two buys you about 0.6%. Minute for minute, Section One is the densest thirty minutes on the paper – and it is also the section two people failed to complete in June.

The consequence

Never overrun Section One, and never abandon it. Every minute past thirty that you spend there is a minute stolen from a section where you are earning at a slower rate – and every Section One question left unwritten forfeits the most efficient marks in the booklet. Twelve marks abandoned in Section One costs you 15% of the written exam. That is the difference between a B and a high C, decided by a clock rather than by anything you know.

This is Priority 1 from your Semester One feedback restated with the arithmetic attached. It has not changed and it is still the highest-leverage fix available to most of you.

The real change: you can no longer ride one text

This is the single most important strategic difference between June and now, and it is worth planning around this week rather than discovering in the exam room.

Semester One · Unit 3 only
Some of the strongest papers in the cohort answered all four questions on Spirited Away alone. That worked, and it was good strategy: every question on the paper was an Art Film question, so one deeply known text could carry the lot. Depth beat breadth, decisively.
Why it worked: the whole paper sat inside a single unit.
Semester Two · the full course
A paper drawing on both units can put an Art Film question and a propaganda question in front of you in the same section. Spirited Away cannot answer a question about agenda setting, and Bowling for Columbine cannot answer one about media art conventions. You will need more than one text – and the choosing has to be instant.
What changes: depth still beats breadth within a response. Across the paper, you now need range.
Your minimum viable portfolio
Four texts, deeply known, covering the whole course between them

Not four texts you have seen. Four texts you could write 1,200 words on without notes. Most of you already have three of these and are one short.

  • One art-film / media-art text – Samson and Delilah or Spirited Away. Carries auteur, aesthetics, themes, representation, cultural context, narrative manipulation, niche audiences, independent production.
  • One documentary / persuasion text – Bowling for Columbine or Waltz with Bashir. Carries point of view, selection and omission, bias, propaganda, ideology, issue construction, editorial control, distribution.
  • Your investigation auteur – the one you researched yourself. You know this director better than I do, and in June the investigation responses produced two of the cohort’s strongest answers. Do not leave it at home.
  • Your government campaign – the one text purpose-built for propaganda, agenda setting, naturalised stereotypes, persuasive representation and the fear–security appeal. It is short, it is dense, and it is the easiest text on your list to revise properly in an evening.

The Film Club texts – Easy Rider, Donnie Darko, Memento – are extensions, not substitutes. Use them if you know one of them genuinely well, particularly for narrative manipulation and media trends. Do not reach for one in an exam because it sounds more sophisticated.

The ten minutes of reading time

You cannot write during reading time. You can do everything else, and the students who use it properly have already made four decisions before the pen moves.

1
Read all ten questions. All of them.Minutes 0–4
Including the ones you are sure you will not do. In June, not one person in the cohort attempted the values question in Section One – a question whose content overlapped almost entirely with the cultural-context and audience questions you did attempt. A question you have pre-emptively ruled out is a question you have not read properly.
2
In Section Two, read all four bullets before judging a questionMinutes 4–7
The first bullet tells you the topic. The last two tell you whether you can actually score. A question whose opening looks familiar can turn on an Evaluate bullet you have no theory for – and a question whose opening looks awkward can carry an Analyse bullet you happen to be perfectly equipped for. Judge a Section Two question by its 8-mark bullets, not its 3-mark one.
3
Attach a text and a scene to each question you might takeMinutes 7–9
Not “I’d do Bowling for Columbine” – that is a text, not a plan. “Columbine, the Heston edit, selection and omission.” If you cannot name the scene during reading time, that is the question to leave. The Route Planner in Part 11 exists so that this decision is already made before you walk in.
4
Commit, and fix the orderMinute 9–10
Four questions, chosen. Then decide the order. Most students should write Section One first, to time, and then move – it protects the densest marks on the paper and it warms up the analytical machinery cheaply. If you know you run long on extended responses, start with your stronger Section Two question, because a strong answer written late is worth less than a strong answer written on a full tank.
Changing your mind

Switching questions at minute forty is what turns a good paper into an unfinished one. If you must switch, do it inside the first five minutes of writing, and never after you have completed a bullet. A B-grade answer to your second-choice question beats an abandoned A-grade answer to your first.

The whole-paper time budget

One hundred and fifty minutes, four responses, and a fifteen-minute buffer you should plan to never need. Print this and rehearse to it, because a time budget you have not practised is a wish.

Semester One exam · Wartime propaganda · Journal Entry 6
2Naming a topic where the question asks for a conceptStill open

The most expensive single habit on Journal Entry 6, and it appeared in every bullet. Gun violence is an issue, not an ideology. Gun ownership is a practice, not a value. Equality and truth are virtues, not the values a documentary contests. The paragraph looks like it has done the job because a concept-shaped noun is sitting in the concept-shaped slot.

Use the strike test. Delete the word “ideology” or “value” from your sentence and read what is left. If the sentence collapses, you named a topic. Semester Two adds more concepts to get wrong – bias, agenda setting, point of view, cultural context, representation – so the test matters more, not less. The interactive sorter is in the JE6 guide.

Journal Entry 6 · habit 1
3Four bullets written as four separate questionsStill open

The four bullets are one argument that builds. The point of view you analyse in bullet 3 should be the position you established in bullet 1. The values you evaluate in bullet 4 should be the ones you defined in bullet 2. Where a response treated each bullet as a standalone task, the later criteria had nothing to attach to and could not reach the upper bands however well written they were.

This is structural, it costs marks on the two eight-mark bullets rather than the cheap ones, and it is fixed in the five minutes of planning – not in the writing.

4Ruling out question types, and leaving marks unattemptedStill open

Three separate instances. In the Semester One exam, two papers completed only one of the two required Section One questions and not a single student attempted the values question. 5Evidence that points at construction instead of producing itImproving

In June this was “move your evidence from plot to scene”. By Journal Entry 6 it had sharpened into the three levels, and the diagnosis got more precise: most of you sit at level two and think you are at level three. Level one is what happens. Level two is what a person or thing looks like, with an effect attached. Level three is what the producer chose – where to film, what to keep in frame, when to cut, what to leave in the audio.

Related and equally common: naming the container instead of the instance. “The disturbing imagery”, “archival footage”, “the animation”, “satire” – all containers. The marks are for what is inside them.

Semester One exam priority 5 · Journal Entry 6 part 3
6Hall’s encoding / decoding – direction, typology and applicationImproving

In June, six of ten papers referenced Hall and only one applied the full typology accurately; several reversed the direction and had the audience encoding. By Journal Entry 6 the cohort used it markedly better. Two things still leak marks.

First, oppositional does not mean “reads it differently” or “gets something else out of it” – that is closer to negotiated. Oppositional means the viewer has decoded the preferred meaning perfectly accurately and refused it. Naming a theory you have misdefined costs more than not naming it. Second, apply it rather than cite it: a sentence that says “according to Hall’s theory” and then stops has gestured at theory, not used it.

Semester One exam priority 3 · Journal Entry 6 bullet 4
7The missing middle – technique named, effect asserted, mechanism absentImproving

The diagnosis from the propaganda task, and it generalises to every analytical paragraph you will write. You name what is in the media work, you state what the audience feels, and you leave out the step in between that explains why one produces the other. “This creates fear.” True, and unmarkable.

The drill: after every observation, force yourself to write “which means…” or “so the viewer…” before you are allowed to name the emotion. If you cannot complete that sentence, you have recognised a technique rather than understood it. And there is a second finding underneath it: if you cannot say how a technique works, you have nothing to weigh when asked how well it works. Priority 1 and priority 7 are the same problem at two altitudes.

Wartime propaganda · the diagnosis
8Claiming all audiences decode identically – and reaching for superlativesStill open

“Moore convinces the audience that…” · “this makes audiences want to change society” · “a masterpiece” · “perfectly” · “flawless”. Each of these claims that every viewer reads the work the same way, which is the opposite of what an Evaluate criterion asks for – and superlatives actively weaken evaluative writing, because evaluation lives in nuance and trade-offs.

The test: if a sentence in your final paragraph could not survive the question “which audience?”, rewrite it.

Semester One exam priority 6 · Journal Entry 6 bullet 4
9Precision of terminology – and using the right name for the right thingImproving

Two marks in every Section One question, and one of the three things that caps your band on every eight-mark bullet. In June the slip was descriptive stand-ins: “back of frame” for background or deep focus, “shot from low down” for a low-angle shot, “music” for non-diegetic score. In the propaganda task it was mislabelling: “call to action”, “direct address” and “mobilisation in a crisis” are not propaganda techniques, and in every case the persuasion had been correctly identified and simply given the wrong name.

Both are cheap to fix and both are free marks. If you can replace a phrase with a more technical one, do it. If a question names a category – symbolic codes, technical codes, written codes – use its vocabulary explicitly.

What the marking key actually says

This is the ratified ladder from the 2025 ATAR examination marking key, not a paraphrase. Every rung combines three things – the cognitive verb, the quality of evidence, and the terminology register – and the weakest of the three caps your mark. You cannot compensate for a thin judgement with detailed examples, and you cannot compensate for vague examples with impressive vocabulary.

Why Journal Entry 6 told you the ladders sit one rung apart

The verb rungs on Analyse and Evaluate are identical. What differs is the object the verb is pointed at. On Analyse, the object is construction – how the producer built the thing. On Evaluate, the object is effect and worth – whether it worked, on whom, and what that tells us.

So when you answer an Evaluate bullet with a second Analyse paragraph, you are not writing at a lower standard – you are writing beautifully about the wrong object, and the descriptor cannot reward it. The mechanism-writing that would have earned a 6 or 7 under Analyse lands two or three rungs down under Evaluate, because the marker is looking for a judgement and finding an explanation. That is the “one rung lower” penalty, and it is applied before the marker has assessed anything else.

The diagnostic, run it over every final paragraph: does this contain a judgement, or only a mechanism?

The five things an 8/8 Evaluate contains

Not a formula to pad out – a checklist of what the top band has that the middle band lacks. Most of you produce one or two of these. The top band has all five, woven rather than listed.

Component 1
An explicit verdict, stated early
Not “there are many readings” but “this works, and here is how well, and here is on whom”. Open the paragraph with a sentence a marker could disagree with. If your opening sentence is uncontroversial, you have written an introduction to an analysis.
Sounds like: “Thornton’s refusal of score is the most effective element of the film’s challenge to Anglo-Australian assumptions – but only for a viewer prepared to sit inside discomfort rather than resolve it.”
Component 2
A criterion – effective at what?
A judgement with no yardstick is an opinion. Name what you are measuring against: the producer’s stated agenda, the values of the intended audience, the account the work sets out to contest, or what the work achieved in the world.
Sounds like: “Measured against Moore’s own stated purpose – relocating the cause of gun violence from individual pathology to a manufactured culture of fear – the casting strategy succeeds and the Heston sequence undermines him.”
Component 3
Differentiated audiences, each on different evidence
Three reading positions supported by the same example is not differentiation – it is one point said three times. Each position needs its own material from the media work. This is what the upper bands are actually checking for, and it is the most common reason a full typology still caps at six.
In Columbine: dominant stands on the casting pattern; negotiated on the Kayla Rolland sequence; oppositional on the omissions and the record outside the film.
Component 4
Theory applied, not cited
A sentence that says “according to Hall’s theory” and then stops has gestured at theory. Applied means the theory is doing work the sentence could not do without it – explaining why two audiences diverge, not labelling the fact that they do.
Cited: “This relates to Hall’s encoding/decoding model.” Applied: “Because the preferred meaning is encoded in a cultural register this audience does not hold, their decoding is negotiated by default rather than by choice.”
Component 5
A limit – the thing your own judgement cannot hold
Evaluation lives in trade-offs. State the condition under which your verdict fails, the audience it does not reach, or the cost the technique carries. A paragraph with no limit in it has probably not evaluated. This is also why superlatives hurt you: “flawless” forecloses exactly the move the band descriptor is looking for.
Sounds like: “…though the same refusal to explain leaves the film vulnerable to the reading it least wants.”

The same paragraph at 4, 6 and 8

One real past bullet, one text you all know, three versions. Same film, same scenes, same knowledge – three very different marks. Read the 4 first and find your own writing in it.

The bullet · 8 marks · 2025 WACE, Question 9

Evaluate how media work can challenge or reinforce audience values.

Worked on Samson and Delilah (Warwick Thornton, Australia, 2009).

Samson and Delilah challenges the values of its Australian audience. Thornton shows the reality of life in a remote Indigenous community, including petrol sniffing, poverty and violence, which many Australians would not have seen before. He uses long takes, natural lighting and very little music so that the film feels realistic rather than dramatic.

This challenges the audience’s values because most Australians believe that everyone in this country gets a fair go, and the film shows that this is not true for everyone. The audience is made uncomfortable and has to watch things they would usually look away from. Thornton also does not use a voiceover to explain anything, so the audience has to work it out themselves.

Because of this, the audience leaves the film with a changed understanding of Indigenous disadvantage in Australia and questions their own values about equality and the fair go.

Why this is a 4. There is evidence from the media work and the terminology is relevant, so it clears the bottom of the ladder comfortably. But every sentence describes an effect rather than judging one. “The audience” is a single undifferentiated body. No theory. No criterion – effective measured against what? And the final sentence is the universal-decodability trap in its purest form: it claims that every viewer leaves changed, which is precisely what an Evaluate criterion exists to interrogate.

The one-sentence diagnosis: this is a competent Analyse paragraph that has wandered into the wrong bullet.

The value Samson and Delilah places under pressure is the “fair go” – the belief that opportunity in Australia is evenly distributed and that hardship is therefore earned. Thornton encodes a challenge to that belief through form rather than statement: the opening cycle of Samson waking, reaching for the petrol tin and drifting through the day is held in natural light and unbroken long takes with no non-diegetic score, denying the audience the cinematic distance that would let them observe addiction from outside it.

Applying Hall’s encoding/decoding model, audiences decode this from different positions. A non-Indigenous metropolitan audience is positioned to take the dominant reading, accepting the structural account of disadvantage the film encodes. An Indigenous audience produces a negotiated reading, recognising the experience represented and reading the film’s value differently – less as revelation than as recognition. A viewer committed to an individual-responsibility account of the same events produces an oppositional reading, understanding the preferred meaning and rejecting it.

The film therefore challenges the values of some audiences and reinforces those of others, depending on the cultural position from which it is decoded.

Why this is a 6. Real progress: the value is named precisely rather than gestured at, the evidence is at construction level, Hall’s direction is correct and all three positions appear. This is a better paragraph than anything the cohort produced in June.

What caps it. It demonstrates the typology rather than judging with it. The three positions are asserted in sequence and lean on the same single piece of evidence – the opening sequence – so they are one observation stated three times rather than three differentiated readings. There is no verdict, no criterion, and no limit. The closing sentence summarises rather than concludes.

Samson and Delilah mounts its challenge to Anglo-Australian values through withholding rather than argument, and the effectiveness of that strategy is almost entirely conditional on what a viewer is prepared to tolerate. Measured against Thornton’s own agenda – to render remote Indigenous experience legible on its own terms rather than translated into the visual rhetoric of mainstream Australian cinema – the refusal is the most effective decision in the film and simultaneously its greatest risk.

The value under pressure is the “fair go”: the belief that opportunity here is evenly distributed and that hardship is therefore earned. For a non-Indigenous metropolitan audience arriving with that belief intact, the dominant encoding lands with real force, and it lands through duration. The opening cycle – Samson waking, the petrol tin, the radio, the wind through the room, held in natural light and unbroken takes with no score to instruct feeling – denies the audience the cinematic distance that would let them observe addiction as a subject. Hall’s model explains why this is more effective than an expository version would be: the preferred meaning is encoded in form rather than content, so a viewer cannot decline the argument without declining the experience of the film itself.

An Indigenous audience, and any viewer already holding a structural account of remote disadvantage, decodes the same material from a different position, and on different evidence. The pull-focus on Delilah watching two lighter-skinned schoolgirls with ice creams is not a revelation to that audience but a recognition; the film’s worth for them lies less in challenging their values than in the rarity of seeing those values held on screen without translation or apology. The negotiated reading is not the lesser reading here – it is the one Thornton’s Kaytetye authorship is addressed to.

The oppositional position is where the evaluation has to be honest. A viewer committed to an individual-responsibility account can watch the entire film and find nothing that contradicts them, precisely because Thornton has stripped out the apparatus – voiceover, explanatory dialogue, a redemptive arc – that would have made the structural argument explicit. The aesthetic strategy that makes the challenge unanswerable for one audience makes it invisible to another. That is the limit of the method, and the closing washing scene suggests Thornton accepts the cost: Delilah bathing an incapacitated Samson in a trough, in flat natural light, resolves nothing and restores no one – refusing the reassurance that might have converted the resistant viewer at the price of the argument.

The judgement therefore has to be split. As a challenge to the values of an audience prepared to sit inside discomfort, the film is extraordinarily effective, and its reception is the evidence: an almost wordless Australian feature, largely without dialogue, taking the Caméra d’Or at Cannes and reaching a domestic audience it was never commercially designed for. As a challenge to the values of an audience that arrived unwilling, it is close to inert. Thornton changed what Australian cinema was permitted to look like more than he changed what Australians believed – and those are two different achievements.

What makes this an 8. All five components, woven rather than listed. A verdict in sentence one that a marker could argue with. An explicit criterion – the director’s agenda – so the judgement has a yardstick. Three reading positions each standing on its own evidence: the opening cycle, the ice-cream pull-focus, the stripped-out apparatus. Hall doing work no other sentence could do, explaining why form-encoding resists refusal. And a genuine limit, followed by the move that separates the top of the band from the rest – splitting one vague success claim into two achievements and rating them separately.

Notice what is not here. No plot summary. No superlatives. No sentence that begins “the audience”. Nothing that could not survive the question “which audience?”

Four moves that take a 7 to an 8

Nobody in this cohort has made any of these yet. They are not difficult – they are just not habits. Each one is a single sentence you can write into a paragraph you have already planned.

Move 1 · the exception
Establish a rule, then find where your own rule breaks
Identifying the point at which your own argument fails is a stronger evaluative move than applying the argument, and it reads as sophistication rather than as inconsistency.
Worked: Moore’s manipulation lies in arrangement rather than invention – genuine footage ordered suggestively. But the Heston sequence is where that distinction collapses: clips assembled from seven separate sentences, recorded a year later at a different event, are not authentic material arranged – they are an utterance Heston never delivered.
Move 2 · split the claim
Turn one vague success into two achievements and rate them separately
“It was effective” is one claim doing too much work. Shifting how an issue is discussed and shifting what people believe are different accomplishments – and a work can manage one without the other.
Worked: “It changed the discourse; it did not change the attitudes.” Or, on an art film: it changed what the national cinema was permitted to look like more than it changed what audiences believed.
Move 3 · the cost of the method
Show that the strategy carries a risk built into it
The strongest evaluations argue that the technique’s power and its vulnerability are the same property. This is not hedging – it is the most precise thing you can say about a construction.
Worked: the refusal to explain makes the challenge unanswerable for one audience and invisible to another. Or: Moore’s persuasive power depends on an under-informed viewer – precisely the audience condition the film attacks the mass media for producing.
Move 4 · fold it back
Take the resistant reading seriously, then return it to the design
Raise the strongest objection to the work, and then argue that the work anticipated it. Done well this is the most sophisticated shape available in a Section Two answer.
Worked: the aesthetic excess of the No-Face sequence risks aestheticising the very consumption it critiques – yet the viewer’s pleasure in the spectacle is precisely what Miyazaki is asking them to refuse, so the implication is the design.

Sentence frames – scaffolding to discard later

Use these until they stop feeling like scaffolding. Then stop using them.

  • Verdict: “Measured against [criterion], the most effective element of [work] is [choice], because…”
  • Differentiation: “For an audience that [holds this value / knows this context], [specific evidence] positions them to… For a viewer who [different position], the same [evidence] reads as…”
  • Theory applied: “Hall’s model explains why these readings diverge: the preferred meaning is encoded in [register], which this audience [does / does not] share, so their decoding is [negotiated / oppositional] by default rather than by choice.”
  • Oppositional, correctly: “This viewer understands the preferred meaning perfectly well and refuses it, decoding from [contrary frame] – and may leave with their position hardened, because…”
  • Limit: “…though for a viewer already [committed / informed], the technique adds little, and [alternative element] does the more useful work.”
  • Both directions, on a “challenge or reinforce” bullet: “While [element] challenges [value], [other element] simultaneously reinforces [related value], and the work’s effect is the tension between them.”
  • Split achievement: “[Work] succeeded in [achievement A]; it has been markedly less successful at [achievement B]. Those are different accomplishments and should be rated separately.”
Four sentences that cap you where it costs most

“Moore convinces the audience that…” · “this makes audiences want to change society” · “a masterpiece” · “this perfectly demonstrates…”

Each claims that every viewer decodes identically, or forecloses the trade-off the criterion is built to reward. Run the test on every sentence of your final paragraph: could this survive the question “which audience?” If not, rewrite it.

And one more, specific to “challenge or reinforce” bullets: argue both. The strongest Evaluate in the cohort’s Journal Entry 6 work did exactly this – a conventional warm ending reinforcing expectation while the substance of the resolution subverted it.

The ninety-second choosing decision

Five questions, you take two. Getting this wrong costs more than any single analytical weakness, and it is decided in the first ninety seconds of reading time.

Ask first
Which question can I attach a scene to, not a text?
“I’d do Bowling for Columbine” is a text. “Columbine – the Nichols kitchen interview – selection and framing” is an answer waiting to be written. If no scene arrives within fifteen seconds, move on.
Ask second
Can I define the concept in the question in one clean sentence?

Ask third
Does my second choice use a different text?
Not compulsory, but useful. Two responses on the same text risks repeating the same scene analysis twice and reads thinner than it is. If both your questions genuinely suit one text, use it – but change the scene.
Do not ask

The topic you enjoy and the topic you have dense, scene-level evidence for are frequently not the same topic. The marker cannot award enthusiasm.

The production question – the one nobody has written

Section One can ask you about your own production. It did in 2024. The syllabus carries production in both units, so it is always available – and almost none of you has ever written a response about your own film under exam conditions. This is the cheapest twelve marks on the paper for a student who has rehearsed it once, and one of the most expensive for a student who meets it cold.

The question, as it has actually been set
“Discuss how the exploration of ideas from other media work could inform your own production.”

2024 WACE ATAR examination, Section One, Question 1. Variants ask about the style, approach or techniques of other works informing your decisions.

Why you should want this question

You own the evidence. On every other question in the paper, you are reconstructing someone else’s film from memory and can be wrong about a year, a shot or a name. Here the primary text is your own Art Film. You made every decision in it. Nobody can catch you out on detail, and “detailed evidence that effectively supports the response” is simply a matter of remembering what you did and why.

It is also the question most likely to be under-attempted by the rest of the state.

The two traps

1 · Writing a production diary. “We filmed at the jetty on the Saturday because the weather cleared” is logistics. The question is about influence and decision-making, not schedule. Nothing about who held the boom earns a mark.

2 · Influence named at brand level. “We were inspired by Wes Anderson’s style” is the container problem again. Which element? The symmetrical framing? The 1.37:1 ratio? The flat frontal staging? Name the influence at code level or it is not evidence.

The four-part shape

1
Name what you were trying to constructContent · Terminology
Start with the meaning, not the film. “My production set out to construct [theme] through [approach].” This is the equivalent of framing the concept, and it gives everything that follows something to be measured against.
2
Name the influence at code level, in a named workEvidence
Title, director, year – then the specific formal solution you took from it. Not “Thornton’s realism” but “Thornton’s use of unbroken long takes with no non-diegetic score across a routine domestic action”. You are evidencing two media works at once here, which is why this question can fill the Evidence column so easily.
3
Say what you took – and what you changedContent · the marks live here
This is the move that separates a 6 from a 3. Influence is not imitation. Explain the adaptation and the reason for it: what your context, budget, location or audience required that the source work did not. A student who can say “I kept the duration but abandoned the natural light because our location gave us no usable window” is demonstrating exactly the decision-making the criterion is built to find.
4
Land what it constructed for your audienceContent
Close on meaning, not on process. What did the borrowed technique do to a viewer of your film? Bring in your intended audience explicitly. If you can name a response you observed at a screening, use it – that is genuine reception evidence and almost nobody thinks to include it.
Worked example · written as if by a student whose Art Film dealt with routine and isolationTarget: 12/12

[1] Exploring other media work informs a production not through imitation but through the transfer of a formal solution – a technique one producer used to solve a problem of meaning, understood well enough to be redeployed in a different context. My Art Film set out to construct isolation as a condition of routine rather than of dramatic event, and both of the works that most directly informed it were chosen because they had solved that same problem.

[2] The primary influence was Warwick Thornton’s Samson and Delilah (Australia, 2009), and specifically the opening cycle: a domestic morning routine held in unbroken long takes, lit naturally, with no non-diegetic score and extended stretches of diegetic silence. Thornton’s decision is not stylistic decoration – the duration is the argument, because it denies the audience the cinematic distance that a cut or a musical cue would restore. The second was Miyazaki’s use of ma in Spirited Away (Japan, 2001), where a five-minute train sequence carrying no plot information is retained rather than compressed.

[3] What I took from Thornton was the refusal to cut: my central sequence holds a single wide static shot of my subject making and eating breakfast for just under ninety seconds, where my instinct had been to build it as a montage. What I changed was the lighting. Thornton’s natural light depends on Central Australian sun through open structures; our location was a suburban kitchen with a single north-facing window, and shooting it naturally produced an underexposed image that read as gloomy rather than ordinary. I introduced a single bounced practical to lift the shadow side, accepting a slight artificiality in exchange for the flat, unremarkable quality the scene needed. From Miyazaki I took the principle rather than the technique – that an empty passage can carry weight – and applied it to a forty-second hold on an empty room after my subject leaves frame.

[4] For my intended audience – a school-age cohort trained on fast-cut, score-driven short-form content – these borrowed decisions construct isolation as something endured rather than performed. At our screening, the ninety-second breakfast produced visible discomfort in the first thirty seconds and stillness after it, which is the shift from watching a scene to inhabiting a duration that Thornton’s opening produces at feature length. The influence was not in what my film looks like; it was in what it refuses to do.

What this does: defines influence conceptually before naming a single film; evidences two media works at code level; distinguishes what was taken from what was adapted, with a reason rooted in production constraint; and closes on constructed meaning for a named audience with observed reception. No logistics, no brand-level influence, no plot summary of either source.

Substitute your own production for the specifics. The four-part shape is the transferable thing – and your influences may well be works we never studied in class, which is entirely legitimate.

Do this before the mock

Spend twenty minutes writing a one-page dossier on your own production: the theme you constructed, three named influences at code level, one decision you adapted and why, your intended audience, and one observed response. That page is a fully-formed twelve-mark answer waiting to be written out, and it will take you twenty minutes now instead of fifteen panicked ones in the exam room.

The rule that decides the upper bands

The one-argument rule

The four bullets are not four questions. They are one argument that builds. The point of view you analyse in bullet 3 should be the position you established in bullet 1. The values you evaluate in bullet 4 should be the ones you defined in bullet 2. The scene you read at code level in bullet 3 should be one you can return to, from a different angle, in bullet 4.

Where a response treats each bullet as standalone, the later criteria have nothing to attach to and cannot reach the upper bands however well written they are. This was the second most expensive habit on Journal Entry 6 and it is fixed in the five minutes of planning, not in the writing.

What the plan looks like
Five minutes, four lines, one spine
  • The object. Which media work, and which specific thing in it the question is really about (the ideology, the point of view, the representation, the aesthetic system).
  • The concept. The definition you will write in bullet 2, in four features, in the abstract.
  • The claim. One analytical sentence about how the work is built. This becomes the opening of bullet 3 and it is what turns a list of examples into a thesis.
  • The verdict + the audiences. Your judgement, and the two or three reading positions, with a different piece of evidence noted beside each.

Students who skip the plan average four to five marks lower on Section Two. Five minutes here saves twenty minutes of mid-sentence redirection later.

The test

Could this paragraph preface any other question? If it could, it is a synopsis, not an overview. A real overview could only introduce this question.

What to include

Title, director, year, country of origin – and where relevant, the year and country represented in the work, the form or mode, and the intended audience. Then the question’s concept, named, with the relationship stated. The 2025 marking key is explicit that the overview should present specific content from the media work that relates to the question’s concept – a scene or a governing theme, not a plot outline.

Two ways to lose it

  • Naming the subject matter instead of the concept. “The ideology of American gun violence” – gun violence is a phenomenon. Run the strike test over your opening sentence.
  • Attributing to the director rather than the media work. “Michael Moore challenges…” moves the frame off construction at exactly the point you should be establishing it – and it predicts the evidence problem that follows in bullets 3 and 4. Make the constructed object the subject of your sentence.

Plot-dumping – the other failure

“Spirited Away is a 2001 Studio Ghibli film about a young girl who gets trapped in a spirit world after her parents are turned into pigs” is a synopsis. None of it serves any question. Notice that the 3/3 exemplars in the Semester One feedback do not mention Chihiro’s parents at all.

The three levels of evidence

The most useful distinction in your Journal Entry 6 feedback, and most of the cohort sat at level two believing they were at level three.

  • Level 1 · plot recall. What happens. Not evidence for a construction criterion at all.
  • Level 2 · content recall. What something looks like, with an effect attached. Describing a person, not a producer’s decisions.
  • Level 3 · construction. What was chosen, what was left out, what was placed next to what, how it is framed and cut. This is where the marks are.

Four questions to run over every example: What did the producer choose? What did they leave out? What did they put next to what? How is it framed and cut? If your sentence answers none of them, you are at level 1 or 2 – however confident it sounds.

Breadth of instance beats breadth of technique

A response that names six techniques with no located example scores badly. A response that pursues one technique family across six located examples, and then says what the pattern adds up to, can score at the very top.

The 7→8 move – still unclaimed in this cohort

State what the pattern of your examples amounts to. Several instances plus a structural claim beats several instances alone, because it turns a list into a thesis.

The worked case: every pro-gun interviewee in Bowling for Columbine is constructed as unstable or foolish and every anti-gun voice as composed and reasonable – “that is not six separate decisions, it is a single sustained casting strategy.” Nobody in the cohort made that move. It is the cleanest available route from a 7 to an 8, and the same shape works on any text: a colour system tracked across a whole film, a framing habit applied only to one group, a sound strategy that appears at every point of moral pressure.

The 6→7 move

Read the frame for the evidence of its own construction. Not recall about the film – a detail visible on screen that exposes a producer’s decision. Heston’s suit changing between shots inside what is presented as one address. The seam is the evidence.

Being persuaded instead of analysing

Reporting a work’s own rhetorical self-presentation as fact – that a documentary “evaluates from both sides”, or presents “only facts, not opinions” – is evidence that the technique worked on you. Noticing how the impression is built is the analysis. A cut does not confirm an encounter is legitimate; it manufactures the appearance of confirmation. That is the observation the criterion rewards.

Part Eight

Text dossiers

Everything you own, with what it is good for and what it is not. Each dossier carries the context, the producer’s agenda, three scene anchors read at construction level, the themes and values, the audience readings, and the dot points it serves best. The last two entries are yours to fill in.

The discipline these are built to enforce

The Semester One feedback’s checklist for every text you intend to use: director’s full name and year; the director’s agenda with evidence from interviews or industry context; cultural and production context; a specifically profiled target audience; audience response across the full Hall typology; themes and values, distinguished from effects; narrative structure; key technologies and stylistic techniques; aesthetic features linked to genre, representation and value systems; at least two key scenes you can read in detail; and a media theory you can apply, not just name.

An effect is not a theme. The reflective stillness produced by ma is an effect. Greed, consumerism, environmentalism and female agency are themes. When a question asks for themes, name actual themes.

Part Nine

The media theory bank

Ten theories, each with the definition, the common error, and – the part that actually earns marks – a sentence showing it applied rather than cited. Two questions in every recent paper have asked for a theory by name. You need three you can use properly, not ten you can list.

Cited versus applied

Cited: “This relates to Hall’s encoding/decoding model.” A sentence that names a theory and then stops has gestured at it.

Applied: the theory does work the sentence could not do without it – explaining why two audiences diverge, not labelling the fact that they do. If you could delete the theorist’s name and the sentence would mean exactly the same thing, you have cited.

And the reminder from the carry-forward board: naming a theory you have misdefined costs more than not naming one at all.

Mock → WACE
Act on the feedback in the first week, not the last
The Semester Two exam guide will go up on the Revision Hub with your paper marked, moderated and turned into priorities – the same treatment the Semester One paper got. The students who improve most are, without exception, the ones who act on that feedback within the first week rather than the last. History is firmly on your side here: almost every year this cohort improves markedly between the Semester One exam and the WACE exam, and students sitting mid-range now routinely climb into the upper bands by November. Not through luck. Through exactly this.

Last thing
The path is the same whether you are moving from a C to a B, a B to an A, or an A to something higher still.

Deepen the concepts. Practise applying them to specific scenes. Attempt past questions under timed conditions. Submit practice responses and act on what comes back. There is no version of this that does not involve writing.

You already have the proof that it works. In June, Describe was the most predictable source of lost marks in your paper. By Journal Entry 6 it was your strongest criterion – and nothing changed except that you worked on it. Evaluate is the same problem with a different name, and there is more of it.

The mock is a rehearsal. November is the performance. Everything on this page is about closing the gap between the two, and the weeks between now and then are a great deal of time if you spend them writing rather than reading.
