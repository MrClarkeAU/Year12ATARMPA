Unit 4 · Power and Persuasion · Task 4

Wartime Propaganda – Short Responses

Your journal entry marked and moderated. One diagnosis, four fixes, and the sentences from your own cohort that show what the top band actually looks like.

Formative · 18 marks · Year 12 ATAR MPA · Sacred Heart College
A message from Mr Clarke

This one was formative – which is exactly why it is worth reading carefully.

This task does not look like an exam question, and it will not appear in this form in November. Four short responses climbing from 2 marks to 8, ten posters on a single sheet, one lesson to write it – that is a diagnostic, not a rehearsal. So do not read your mark as a prediction of anything.

Read it instead as a very clean X-ray of one specific skill: can you explain how persuasion actually works? Strip away the film knowledge, the theory, the essay structure, and that is all this task tested. It is also the single thing every Unit 4 question – and the Bowling for Columbine essay coming next – is built on top of.

The good news is that as a cohort you can already see the persuasion. You spotted fear, transfer, euphemism and bandwagon accurately and you found real evidence for them. What most of you have not yet built is the habit of explaining the process in between. That is a fixable habit, not a talent, and this page is about how to fix it.

— Mr Clarke

Part 1

The diagnosis – the missing middle

Almost every mark lost on this task, across every question, comes back to one habit. You name what is in the poster, then you state what the audience feels – and you leave out the step in between that explains why one produces the other.

Step 1 · you did this Name the technique and quote the evidence. “Poster 7 uses fear – the caricatures loom over a burning forest.”
Step 2 · the missing middle Explain the process. Why does that image do anything to anyone? What does it make the viewer think, assume or fear?
Step 3 · you did this State the outcome. “This creates fear and encourages hate towards Germany and Japan.”
What assertion sounds like

These are all real sentences from this cohort’s papers. Every one of them jumps from evidence straight to outcome:

“This creates fear.”  ·  “The swastika is a key symbol used to revive feelings of patriotism.”  ·  “This simple yet effective slogan might make the viewer evoke patriotism.”  ·  “The depiction of flags and military attire persuades audiences to believe the Nazi regime is superior.”

Each one is true. None of them is analysis. A marker cannot award the explanation marks for a claim that the poster simply works.

What the process sounds like

Also from this cohort. Notice that each one has a middle:

“The intentional selection of softer words like ‘stop him’ instead of ‘kill him’ … persuades the audience that atrocities committed in war are minimal and necessary.”

“When a group is displayed as a vast majority, it is more likely to cause the viewer to question the righteousness of their own belief, in return for a sense of belonging.”

Softer word → killing reframed as routine work → moral resistance drops. Crowd → conformity pressure → belief traded for belonging. That is the middle.

Why this matters more than the mark

There is a second finding underneath the first. Almost nobody evaluated. Q4 asked you to evaluate, and across the whole cohort no response weighed one technique against another, considered an audience the poster might fail on, or qualified a claim.

Those two findings are the same finding. If you cannot say how a technique works, you have nothing to weigh when asked how well it works. Fix the middle and evaluation becomes available to you. Skip it and your judgements will float free of the evidence.

Part 2

Four fixes, in order of value

Roughly ordered by how many marks each one cost the cohort. Tick each one when you have started working on it – the tracker remembers your progress on this device.

Working on
0 / 4
1
Highest valueEvery question

Write the middle of the chain

The most common single fault in the set. A technique is named, evidence is quoted, and the effect is asserted – with no account of the mechanism connecting them.

After every observation you make, force yourself to write “which means…” or “so the viewer…” before you are allowed to name the emotion. If you cannot complete that sentence, you have not yet understood the technique – you have only recognised it.

2
TerminologyEasiest fix

Name the technique from the repertoire

Three responses built a whole answer on something that is not a propaganda technique – “call to action”, “direct address”, “mobilisation in a crisis”. In every case the persuasion had been identified correctly and simply given the wrong label.

“Direct address” is a mode of address. “Call to action” describes what a poster asks for, not how it persuades. The techniques you needed were sitting on your sheet: fear and selection would each have earned the mark. Learn the eight main techniques and the six guidelines by name – there is a checklist at the bottom of this page.

3
Evaluate · 8 marksBiggest ceiling

Push into judgement

Q4 asked you to evaluate. No response in the cohort reached full marks on that criterion. Explaining what a technique does is analysis; the question wanted you to go a step further.

An evaluation needs three moves: a judgement (“the fear appeal does more of the work than the assertion”), a reason grounded in the poster, and a limit (“though for a viewer already committed to the war it adds little”). Watch for the giveaway – if your paragraph only says what happens to “the audience”, you are still in analysis.

4
StructuralCheap marks

Answer all of it, and read the limits

One paper left Q4 entirely blank and another left Q3 blank – between them, twelve marks unattempted. Two more responses stopped mid-question or collapsed into dot points. Four papers referenced five or six posters when the task said a maximum of four.

Q4 alone is worth 8 of the 18 marks – more than Q1 and Q2 combined. Budget for it before you polish anything else, and if time is short, write four rough sentences rather than none. And take the instruction line seriously: a maximum of four posters is part of the task, not a suggestion.

Cohort mean overall: 13.6 / 18. The spread was wide – from 7 to 17 – and almost all of that spread came from Q4.

Question 1 · cohort average 1.8 / 2
Identify the value, attitude or belief the creators are trying to alter
2 marks
Identify the value, attitude or belief the creators of two posters are trying to alter. (2 marks)

What the question asks

One mark per poster. The trap is the word alter. You are not being asked what the poster shows, what it asks for, or what side it is on – you are being asked what the creator wants to change inside the viewer’s head. A value, an attitude or a belief. Name it as one.

What the strongest responses did

The best answers went past the slogan to the belief underneath it, and some went one step further to what the belief was used to justify. One response read “Long live Germany” as a belief in Aryan racial superiority and then linked that belief to expansionism. Another identified that poster 6 wants a viewer to place a pacifist desire for peace second, behind a “brief and necessary job” – that is a value being displaced, precisely stated.

Where marks were lost

The one slip

Stating the poster’s position instead of the audience’s. “Source nine is anti-Nazi” tells a marker where the poster stands; it does not say what the creator wants the viewer to start or stop believing. Same for answers that only restated a slogan.

Also: a single blended sentence covering both posters cannot earn both marks. Write two sentences, one per poster.

Question 2 · cohort average 3.6 / 4
Explain how two posters used the same propaganda technique
4 marks
Explain how two posters have employed the same propaganda technique to influence their audience. (4 marks)

What the question asks

Four separable things: name one technique common to both, evidence it in poster A, evidence it in poster B, and explain the effect on the audience. Miss any one and you cap yourself at three.

What the strongest responses did

The cohort handled this one securely. Fear was the dominant choice and was generally well evidenced. One response defined transfer properly before applying it, then tracked it through the Nazi flag, the eagle and Hitler’s stature before pivoting across the world to Uncle Sam’s red, white and blue – and made the mechanism explicit, that symbols which alone “may mean a multitude of things” are funnelled back into a single feeling. Another read the two shadowy figures as a “common enemy” and argued that externalising a human into an enemy diminishes the immorality of killing.

Where marks were lost

Terminology, not analysis

Every mark lost on this question was the naming mark, and every one of those was the same mistake: the right observation with the wrong label. “Call to action”, “direct address” and “mobilisation in a crisis” are not propaganda techniques.

Also watch evidence thinness. Quoting a single pronoun – “him” – is not enough to evidence a technique in a poster.

Question 3 · cohort average 3.3 / 4
Contrast the use of symbolic codes in two posters
4 marks
Contrast the use of symbolic codes in two posters. (4 marks)

What the question asks

Symbolic codes only – setting, costume, props, colour, gesture, facial expression, iconography. The command word is contrast, and it carries its own mark. Describing two posters side by side is not contrasting them; you have to name the difference and say what it does.

What the strongest responses did

This produced the two best pieces of writing in the set. One response tracked a single symbol – the swastika – across two posters and argued it appeared in “opposing value and quantity”: concealed once among the reds of an American poster because it is an offensive symbol on American soil, then multiplied across dozens of high-contrast flags on a German street. Same symbol, opposite deployment.

Another pushed the contrast up a level, distinguishing not just two symbols but two kinds of symbol: Uncle Sam a fictional character who stands in for the people, Hitler a real man who became a symbol – and landed it on the emotions each targets, hope against resolve.

The move worth stealing

Both of those answers found a shared axis and then split it. Same symbol, opposite quantity. Same purpose, opposite emotion. That structure builds a contrast automatically – and it is far stronger than “poster A does this, poster B does that.”

Where marks were lost

Running out of road

The largest single loss on this question was an answer that analysed one poster in rich detail, then stopped – the second poster never arrived and no contrast was drawn. If time is tight, write the contrast sentence first and hang your evidence off it.

One paper did not attempt this question at all. Four marks for a rough attempt is always worth taking.

Question 4 · cohort average 4.9 / 8 · where the spread came from
Detailed analysis of one poster, evaluating techniques and codes
8 marks
Provide a detailed analysis of one poster, evaluating the creator’s use of two propaganda techniques and two typical media codes to persuade the audience to change their values, attitudes, beliefs or behaviour. (8 marks)

What the question asks

Count the required parts: two techniques, two media codes, an evaluation, and an explicit link to the value, attitude, belief or behaviour being targeted. Four things, and a plan that gives each of them a share of your time.

What the strongest responses did

The best Q4 answers explained the psychology rather than the poster. One argued that bandwagon works by appealing to “the innate human desire to conform and find safety in numbers”, so that a viewer facing an apparent majority trades the righteousness of their own belief for a sense of belonging. Another read the reduction of soldiering to a “job” as concealing the reality of the role in order to lower the barrier to enlisting.

Note what those two have in common: neither is really about a flag or a slogan. Both are about what happens inside the viewer.

Where marks were lost

Three ways this question leaked marks

1. One technique carried, one abandoned. Several responses developed their first technique properly and then named a second and left it – sometimes mid-sentence. Two half-explained techniques score less than you think.

2. Codes discussed but never named as codes. Crowds, flags, slogans, uniforms and lighting appeared in plenty of answers without ever being called composition, colour, symbolic or written codes. If the question names a category, use its vocabulary.

3. No evaluation at all. The most common ceiling. See fix three above.

Context check

Poster 6 – “Stop him and the job’s done” – is a Second World War poster depicting a Japanese soldier. It was read across the cohort as Vietnamese and as Chinese, and one otherwise excellent answer built its symbolic reading on the star as a communist emblem. If you are not certain of a context, analyse the construction and leave the history out.

Part 4

Exemplar bank – sentences from your own cohort

All of these were written in one lesson by people sitting in your class. Filter by what you want to work on, read the sentence, then read the note on what makes it work – or not.

Mechanism · bandwagon Q4
“The bandwagon technique encourages the viewer to align themselves with the dominantly portrayed view or group by appealing to the innate human desire to conform and find safety in numbers. When a group is displayed as a vast majority, it is more likely to cause the viewer to question the righteousness of their own belief, in return for a sense of belonging or social acceptance.”
The move: this explains the technique, not the poster. Crowd → conformity pressure → self-doubt → belief traded for belonging. Four steps, and the viewer is a person rather than “the audience”. This is the standard the whole cohort should be aiming at.
Mechanism · euphemism Q4
“The intentional selection of softer words like ‘stop him’ instead of ‘kill him’ and ‘job’s done’ instead of ‘killing done’ is utilised to persuade the audience that atrocities committed in war are minimal and necessary.”
The move: the word choice is not just noticed, it is set against the word that was avoided. Naming the alternative the creator rejected is one of the fastest ways to prove a choice was deliberate.
Mechanism · transfer Q2
“The waving flag of Nazi Germany, the eagle shining down from above, the resilient stature of Hitler himself – iconography that on their own may mean a multitude of things – ties it back down to patriotism.”
The move: this response defined transfer before using it, then showed the mechanism – symbols that are individually vague being funnelled into one feeling. Definition first, application second, is a habit worth keeping for the essay.
Contrast · symbolic codes Q3
“A symbol shared between the posters is the swastika … Poster 7 uses it once, subtly, along the hat of a crudely depicted German … concealed in the mix of reds, as it’s an offensive symbol in American territory. Sharing this symbol but in opposing value and quantity is the pro-Nazi poster 10, proudly displaying swastikas on Hitler’s uniform as well as dozens of flags.”
The move: one symbol, two deployments. Finding a shared axis and then splitting it generates the contrast for you – and the reasoning about why it is concealed on American soil is genuine contextual analysis.
Contrast · kinds of symbol Q3
“Whilst Hitler was a real person and the actual leader of Nazi Germany, now also a symbol to inspire his own people, Uncle Sam is a fictional character … instead of representing a leader he represents the people … whilst poster 10 is hopeful … poster 2 is more serious … evoking resolve rather than hope.”
The move: the contrast is pushed to a second level. Not just “these two symbols differ” but “these are different types of symbol, and so they target different emotions.” That is what lifts a contrast into the top band.
Identify · belief and consequence Q1
“‘Long live Germany’ emphasises the belief that ‘pure Germans’, also known as the Aryan race, is far more superior than any other race or religion; therefore they feel encouraged to dominate anyone deemed less superior, which was achieved through expansionism.”
The move: slogan → belief → what the belief was used to justify. A two-mark question does not need this much, but the habit of chasing an idea to its consequence is exactly what the eight-mark questions reward.
Watch out · assertion Q4
“This simple yet effective slogan might make the viewer evoke patriotism as a value and therefore forcing them to consider or join the Nazi Party.”
What is missing: why does a vague, agreeable phrase work? Glittering generalities function precisely because a word like “Germany” means something slightly different to every person reading it, so everyone fills it with their own version. Say that, and the same sentence earns its marks.
Watch out · wrong label Q2
“Both posters emphasise a ‘call to action’. This technique employs significant codes and symbols which aim to persuade an audience to think or act in a certain way.”
What is missing: a real technique. The observation underneath is sound – and the same response went on to explain how “I want you” and “Men!” create a personalised responsibility, which is good work. It just needed a name from the sheet attached to it.
Watch out · position, not belief Q1
“Source nine is anti-Nazi.”
What is missing: the audience. This says where the poster stands, not what it wants a viewer to start or stop believing. One extra clause – “… and works to replace any sense of shared humanity with disgust” – turns it into an answer.
Part 5

Sentence toolkit

Two patterns. Learn them, use them until they stop feeling like scaffolding, then discard the scaffolding.

Pattern 1 · the mechanism sentence

The creator uses [technique], seen in [specific evidence], which works by [the process – what it makes the viewer assume, fear, want or identify with], so that the viewer [the shift in value, attitude, belief or behaviour].

The third clause is the one you have been leaving out. It is also the one carrying the marks.

Pattern 2 · the evaluation sentence

Judgement: Of the two, the fear appeal does more of the persuasive work …

Reason: … because it operates instantly through the image, before the viewer has read a word, whereas the assertion only lands once the slogan has been processed …

Limit: … though for a viewer already committed to enlisting, the fear appeal adds little and the assertion’s promise of a quick, finished “job” is the more useful reassurance.

Judgement, reason, limit. If your paragraph has no limit in it, you have probably not evaluated.

Two habits from the top of this cohort

Name the road not taken. “‘Stop him’ instead of ‘kill him’” proves the choice was deliberate. Ask yourself what the creator could have done and did not.

Find a shared axis, then split it. For any contrast question, look for the one thing both texts have in common and show how each uses it differently. Same symbol, opposite quantity. Same goal, opposite emotion.

Part 6

Technique repertoire – know these by name

Straight from your propaganda techniques sheet. You do not need to memorise definitions word for word, but you do need to be able to name the right one under time pressure and say how it works. Tick each one you could explain to someone else right now.

And the codes, while you are here

Technical codes – framing, camera angle, composition, lighting, focus. Symbolic codes – colour, setting, costume, props, gesture, facial expression, iconography. Written codes – slogan, typography, font size, capitalisation, language choice.

Q4 asked for two typical media codes and several of you supplied them without ever using those words. Name the category. It costs nothing and it is worth marks.
